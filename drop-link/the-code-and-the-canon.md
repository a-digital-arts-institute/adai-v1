# The Code and the Canon

*A response to "How Revolutions Happen in the Art Market"*

---

The diagnosis is sharp: markets announce, archives confirm. When the art market shifts, the money arrives first and the institutional memory follows. This much is right. But the sequence assumes a prior condition that does not yet exist for digital art — that someone, somewhere, knows what they are preserving. Before governance, before canon formation, before the archive can do its retrospective work, there is an ontological question that the field has been deferring for thirty years: what is the original in digital art?

Not the file. A JPEG is a compression artefact, not a work. Not the code alone — code is a score, not a performance. Not the behaviour, exactly, because behaviour depends on hardware, on rendering engines, on network conditions that will never recur. The work might be the experience of it running on specific hardware at a specific moment. It might be the system of relations between code, context, and encounter. This is not philosophy for its own sake. It is the practical problem that makes every governance framework for digital art premature until it is addressed. You cannot preserve what you have not decided how to identify.

---

There is a shrine at Ise, in Japan, that has stood for thirteen centuries. Every twenty years, the entire structure is dismantled and rebuilt on an adjacent site using new materials and traditional techniques. The wood is new. The nails are new. The shrine persists. Continuity at Ise is not a property of matter but a transmission — of form, of function, of the knowledge that makes the form meaningful. The priests who oversee the rebuilding do not merely replicate a structure. They carry forward the reason the structure exists.

Digital art has the same architecture of impermanence. Software degrades not slowly, like paint, but catastrophically — a browser update, a deprecated plugin, a platform closure, and the work is gone. The field's conservation vocabulary reflects this: emulation, migration, reinstantiation. These are technical responses to what is fundamentally a cultural problem. Emulation recreates an execution environment. Migration converts a work to current technologies. Neither asks the question the Ise priests would ask: what is the essential character of this work, and what must be carried forward for it to remain itself?

What digital art lacks is not better emulation. It lacks the equivalent of the priestly tradition — the people, the practices, and the institutional memory that know why the form matters, not just how to copy it. Conservation researchers have been saying this for two decades. The Variable Media approach, formalised in 2003, understood that preserving behaviour matters more than preserving material. Rhizome's ArtBase, the EMBARK network across twenty-five European countries, Tate's time-based media programme — each represents a partial answer. But partial answers in separate institutions do not constitute a field-wide practice of transmission. The knowledge remains siloed, underfunded, and structurally marginal to the institutions that hold the work.

---

The Beeple sale was not a market excess. It was a stress test on absent infrastructure.

The NFT cycle briefly made visible what had always been true: digital art had transaction provenance — who owned what, when, recorded immutably on-chain — but no meaning provenance. No systematic record of what a work was doing conceptually, what field it was in dialogue with, what critical discourse surrounded it, how its significance had changed over time. Ownership provenance was globally deployed on Ethereum, Tezos, and a dozen other chains. Meaning provenance existed nowhere at scale.

When the speculative bubble collapsed — wiping out ninety-five per cent of NFT project value by 2024 — what survived told the story. Art Blocks survived through curatorial rigour. Feral File survived through exhibition quality. Institutional acquisition programmes survived through preservation mandates. What did not survive was everything that had been held aloft by attention alone, because there was no connective tissue beneath it. No critical infrastructure to distinguish significant work from trivial work. No shared vocabulary for discussing what made any of it matter. The field's only filter was the market, and when the market left, the filter went with it.

The CloneX incident in April 2025 made the failure concrete. When RTFKT's servers went dark, thousands of NFTs lost their associated artwork overnight. The tokens persisted on-chain — ownership provenance intact, immutable, technically perfect. But the art was gone. What remained were records of ownership of an absence. The incident proved what the broader collapse had already suggested: ownership provenance without meaning provenance is hollow. You can track who owns what forever, but if no one has tracked what the work means, what it is in dialogue with, where it sits in the field's intellectual history, then the record is a receipt for something no one can describe.

---

The article under discussion lands on a confident conclusion: revolutions are confirmed in archives. But an archive is retrospective. It captures after the fact. It organises what has already been recognised. For a field as established as Impressionism or Abstract Expressionism, this works. The revolution happened, the vocabulary stabilised, the archive collected.

Digital art moves at a different speed and with a different plurality. New practices emerge faster than critical language can name them. Generative AI produced entirely new aesthetic categories in under three years — and the critical vocabulary is already lagging behind the work. The field has no equivalent of Artforum or October dedicated to sustained, rigorous criticism of digital art. Right Click Save operates with limited resources. Most coverage is promotional or market-oriented. Without independent critical voices, market narratives become the default filter for significance. The attention economy fills the vacuum that the archive was supposed to occupy.

What the field actually requires is something anticipatory: infrastructure that tracks meaning as it forms, that can sense when a new vocabulary is emerging before it has a name, that treats the frontier — the unclassifiable, the not-yet-named — as the most valuable signal rather than noise to be tidied up later. This is a different institutional logic from the archive entirely. An archive asks: what happened? The infrastructure digital art needs asks: what is happening, and what does the field not yet have language for?

---

This is the gap that A(DAI) — a digital arts institute, with the indefinite article load-bearing — is designed to address. Not a platform, not a marketplace: a knowledge commons. A semantic graph where practitioner intelligence, lived experience, and field signals accumulate into something the field can query, traverse, and build on.

The operating logic is specific. Every signal carries full provenance — who contributed it, when, how, at what confidence level, under what consent. But the provenance tracked is not ownership. It is meaning: what a work embodies, what it responds to, what techniques it uses, what field it is in conversation with. When multiple works occupy the same conceptual position, that is a tendency. When a work occupies a position no other work shares, that is a frontier. When two works respond to each other but embody opposing ideas, that is a tension — and tensions, not consensus, are what reveal what the field is actually arguing about.

The architecture is commons-first. The data is CC-BY-SA. The processing rules are open, versioned, and forkable — anyone can take the same data, apply different rules, and run a different commons. The governance is architectural, not bureaucratic: the fork right is the structural guarantee against enclosure.

The market gave the field provenance of ownership. It never gave it provenance of meaning. The archive will not build itself. The question is whether the field constructs the institutional memory it needs — patient, anticipatory, rigorous — or continues to rely on the market to tell it what matters. The shrine at Ise has lasted thirteen hundred years not because the wood endures, but because the knowledge of why it matters has been transmitted without interruption. Digital art needs its own tradition of transmission. The wood is already rotting.
