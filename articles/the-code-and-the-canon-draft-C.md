# The Code and the Canon

*Register C — standalone essay, written from A(DAI)'s position*

---

Five years after the most expensive digital artwork sale in history, the question it posed has still not been answered. Not the obvious question — was it worth $69 million — but the structural one underneath: what kind of institution can give digital art historical continuity rather than market volatility?

The sale itself is well known. In March 2021, Vignesh Sundaresan paid $69.3 million at Christie's for a jpeg by Beeple, a collage of 5,000 daily drawings made over thirteen years. The blockchain recorded who owned what, and when. Provenance of ownership, solved at scale, overnight. 22 million people watched the auction's final moments. The art world treated it as either a breakthrough or a warning.

We started building A(DAI) because of what that transaction exposed by contrast. It proved digital art could carry financial weight. And in doing so it made visible everything the field had failed to build around that weight. There was no infrastructure tracking what the work meant conceptually, what field it sat in dialogue with, what critical discourse surrounded it, how its significance might shift over time. The market could say who owned the Beeple. It could not say why it mattered. We call this gap provenance of meaning, and it is what we are trying to build.

**I.**

In the mountains of western Georgia, a vocal tradition has been transmitted without interruption for over a thousand years. Three voices improvise within strict modal rules, weaving a polyphony that is never performed identically twice. There is no score. No conductor. The songs are recognised not by fixed notes but by the logic that governs them: the intervals, the rules of movement between voices, the relationship between parts.

What persists across generations is not a text but a system of knowledge. A master singer does not hand a student a manuscript. He transmits the modal rules, the vocal technique, and the understanding of what each song is for. A harvest song carries different rules from a liturgical one. A mourning song permits intervals that a celebration forbids. Function shapes form.

UNESCO recognised Georgian polyphonic singing as intangible heritage. The word is precise. What is being preserved is not an object but a practice: the living knowledge of people who understand why the form matters, not merely how to reproduce it.

Record the audio and you have an artefact. Lose the singers and you have lost the tradition. Digital art, as we see it, has plenty of recordings and almost no singers.

**II.**

The NFT crash made the absence hard to miss. Monthly trading volumes dropped 97% by late 2022. Over 95% of NFT collections were later assessed as worthless. In April 2025, the CloneX collection, 19,985 avatar NFTs backed by Nike, lost its artwork entirely when Cloudflare cut access to the servers hosting the metadata. The tokens survived on-chain. The art vanished. Holders owned proof of ownership pointing at nothing.

What briefly looked like a revolution in artistic legitimacy turned out to be, in most cases, driven by the oldest mechanism in market history: the fear of missing out. When the speculative energy withdrew, almost no connective tissue remained. Not because the works were not real, but because the relational layer that gives works historical weight — exhibition context, critical discourse, conceptual lineage — had never been built.

The collector and writer Sylvain Levy put it well: the Beeple sale marked a threshold, not a destination. He argues that revolutions are announced in markets and confirmed in archives, and that what follows requires the slower discipline of institutional construction.

We agree with the diagnosis. But it assumes you already know what you are preserving. For digital art, that assumption is where things get difficult. What is the original in a work that exists as code? The file? The behaviour when it runs? The experience of it rendering on specific hardware at a specific moment? This is not philosophy for its own sake. It is the practical problem that makes every governance framework premature until it is answered.

**III.**

Sundaresan's own trajectory gets at this. In a recent CNN profile, he describes the crash as producing a "meaning crash" that left him questioning why he had been so concerned with spectacle over substance. He has opened a gallery in Singapore that does not sell art. He commissions work. He has stopped buying NFTs but insists minting them is still necessary as a mechanism. The man who made the most visible transaction in digital art history is now reaching for something transactions cannot produce.

We recognise that instinct. It is the same one that started this project.

But reaching for it and building it are different things. An archive would help, and Levy is right that the field needs institutional memory. But an archive captures after the fact. It is retrospective by design. What digital art's speed and plurality require is something anticipatory. Infrastructure that tracks meaning as it forms. That can pick up on a new vocabulary emerging before it has a settled name. That treats the frontier, the work that resists existing categories, as the most valuable signal rather than noise to be tidied up later.

This is what the Georgian singers have and what the digital art field does not. A living transmission system. The singers do not archive their tradition. They practise it. The knowledge travels because people carry it, adapt it, pass it on with the reasons intact. New songs get composed within the modal system, but the community that holds the system is what keeps it coherent.

A(DAI) is our attempt at the digital equivalent. A semantic graph, a knowledge commons, where practitioner intelligence, critical discourse, and field signals accumulate into something the field can query and build on. Every node carries provenance: who contributed it, when, how, at what confidence, under what consent.

Think of a drop of water meeting a surface. It never lands the same way twice. Its behaviour depends entirely on what it encounters. Our interface works on the same principle. It reads the relationships surrounding whatever a person encounters and narrates the context accordingly. Three people exploring the same region of the graph each get a different reading, because each arrives carrying different knowledge and different questions. Like the three Georgian voices: improvising within shared rules, never identical, always recognisably part of the same song.

**IV.**

The editorial rules that govern what enters the graph are open, versioned, and forkable. Anyone can take the same data, apply different rules, run a different commons. If we get it wrong, the architecture lets someone else get it right using the same material.

We are building this in public, under CC-BY-SA. A knowledge commons that can be enclosed is not a commons. The data belongs to the field. What it means is the field's to decide.

The market gave the field provenance of ownership. It never gave it provenance of meaning.

The Georgian singers solved this centuries ago. Three voices. No score. Always recognisable. The tradition persists because the people who hold it understand what it is for.

We are building the institution that carries the reasons.
