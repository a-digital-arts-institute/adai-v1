# Before it has a name 

---

In the mountains of western Georgia, a vocal tradition has been transmitted for over a thousand years. Three voices improvise within strict modal rules, weaving a polyphony that is never performed identically twice. There is no score. No conductor. The songs are recognised not by fixed notes but by the logic that governs them: the intervals, the rules of movement between voices, the relationship between parts.

What persists across generations is a system of knowledge. A master singer does not hand a student a manuscript. He transmits the modal rules, the vocal technique, and the understanding of what each song is for. A harvest song carries different rules from a liturgical one. A mourning song permits intervals that a celebration forbids. Function shapes form. The modernist formula ran the other way — form follows function — but the singers know better. The function of a song determines which intervals are permitted, which voice leads, how the others respond. The form is an outcome of the reason for singing.

UNESCO recognised Georgian polyphonic singing as intangible heritage. The word is precise. What is being preserved is not an object but a practice: the living knowledge of people who understand why the form matters, not merely how to reproduce it.

Record the audio and you have an artefact. Lose the singers and you have lost the tradition.

Digital art carries the same structure. A generative work is not its file. It is not the rendering on a particular screen. It is the logic that produces the rendering, the conditions under which the logic was written, the conversation it entered. Emulation, migration, file backup: these are the recordings. The singers exist — the artists, the practitioners, the people doing the work. What is missing is what the Georgian tradition has between its singers: the shared modal knowledge, the transmission structure, the connective tissue that lets one practitioner's reasons become legible to another.

---

Art has always sensed before it named. The Impressionists were not illustrating a theory of light. They were painting what they saw, and the theory came later. The Dadaists were not executing a manifesto. The disgust came first, then the words for it.

Digital art is doing this now, across mediums that have been evolving since the 1960s — algorithmic art, cybernetic sculpture, video, net art, generative code, protocol-based practice — each arriving on its own schedule, often in parallel, rarely in sequence. But the field producing this work has no infrastructure that can hold its complexity, sense what is forming, or carry the reasons forward. It has a medium that keeps evolving, keeps proposing new tools, and outruns the vocabulary meant to describe it every few years. So we ask, what does an institution look like that's built to move with the complexity?

---

Five years ago, a transaction briefly made this visible. Vignesh Sundaresan paid $69.3 million at Christie's for a jpeg by Beeple, a collage of 5,000 daily drawings made over thirteen years. Twenty-two million people watched the auction's final moments — a spectacle Sundaresan himself would later question. The blockchain recorded who owned what, and when. Provenance of ownership, solved at scale, overnight.

The sale proved digital art could carry financial weight. It told us nothing about what the work meant, what field it was in dialogue with, what it was responding to, or how its significance might change over time. The market could say who owned the Beeple. It had little to say about why it mattered.

The crash that followed made the absence impossible to ignore. Monthly trading volumes dropped 97% by late 2022. Over 95% of NFT collections were later assessed as worthless. In April 2025, the CloneX collection, 19,985 avatar NFTs backed by Nike, lost its artwork entirely when Cloudflare cut access to the servers hosting the metadata. The tokens survived on-chain. The art vanished. Holders owned proof of ownership pointing at nothing.

Infrastructure that was built on curatorial rigour survived. Art Blocks, Feral File, fxhash continued operating because they were grounded in artist-led governance rather than speculation. They proved the model works when the foundation is real. What none of them were designed to do, because it was never any single platform's job, was build the connective tissue between them: the shared vocabulary, the relational map, the field-level memory that lets works accumulate meaning over time rather than just circulate. That layer did not collapse in the crash. It had never been built.

Even the institutions that engaged have no system for this. MoMA accessioned eight CryptoPunks into its permanent collection in late 2025. It can preserve the tokens and display the pixel art. It cannot capture what CryptoPunks meant to the communities that made them matter, how that meaning shifted, or where they sit in relation to the broader field.

Take Refik Anadol’s Unsupervised, which MoMA commissioned, exhibited in its lobby for nearly a year, and acquired for the permanent collection. On the blockchain: a token, an owner, a price history. In the museum’s records: a title, a date, a medium description. Nowhere: that the work processes 138,000 images from MoMA’s own collection through a machine learning model. That it shares a lineage with decades of generative and software art, but that no existing system maps the relationship. That it triggered a public argument about whether AI-assisted work belongs in a museum at all. All of this is the work’s meaning provenance. None of it is captured by any existing infrastructure. It lives in scattered reviews, social media threads, and the memories of people who were paying attention. When those fade, the context fades with them.

The problem runs deeper than individual works. Writing in ArtReview, Charlotte Kent observes that Leo Villareal's code-based lightboxes and Jenny Holzer's digital animation displays are filed under "contemporary art," while structurally similar practices are enclosed as "digital art." The dividing line, she suggests, is whether the technology is visible. Art that obscures its computational material gets absorbed into the canon. Art that foregrounds it gets a separate category. The field's own boundaries are drawn by institutional convenience, not by the actual relationships between practices. Meaning provenance is unbuilt. But the map itself is also wrong.

The collector and writer Sylvain Levy has a useful formulation: the Beeple sale marked a threshold, not a destination. He argues that revolutions are announced in markets and confirmed in archives. The diagnosis is right as far as it goes. But an archive assumes you already know what you are preserving. What is the original in a work that exists as code? The file? The behaviour when it runs? The experience of it rendering on specific hardware at a specific moment? An archive also assumes time moves in one direction: something happens, then it is recorded. Meaning in this field doesn't work like that. A practice goes dormant for a decade, then a new tool makes it legible again. Two practitioners in different cities converge on the same problem without knowing the other exists. The vocabulary for what they're doing arrives years after the work. An archive captures after the fact. What the field needs is something that can sense what is forming before it has a name, and sit with it while the name is still missing.

So much for the diagnosis. What follows is a design problem.

---

Traditional art built this slowly. The Getty vocabularies hold 304,000 artist records and 70,000 structured concepts, accumulated over decades. Rhizome has been doing preservation and criticism for born-digital work since 1996, and its ArtBase is the closest thing to institutional memory the field has, but it runs on grants, is editorially bottlenecked, and cannot operate at field scale. Digital art has no shared vocabulary, no relational map, no authority file. Transaction records and fragments.

Sundaresan himself seems to feel the gap. In a recent CNN profile, he describes the crash as producing a "meaning crash" that left him questioning why he had been so concerned with spectacle over substance. He has opened a gallery in Singapore that does not sell art. He commissions work. He insists minting NFTs is still necessary as a mechanism but has stopped buying them. The man who made the most visible transaction in digital art history is reaching for something transactions cannot produce.

A(DAI), a digital arts institute, is an attempt to build that sensing mechanism. A knowledge commons, structured as a semantic graph, where you can see how works, practitioners, and ideas relate to each other and follow the connections. Artists sit at the centre of gravity. Their interactions and contributions form the emergent narrative of the field as it develops. The market logic follows from that, not the other way around.

Think of water. It reads every surface it meets. It finds the cracks, follows gravity to the lowest point, pools where the weight collects. It never lands the same way twice because its behaviour depends entirely on the composition of what it encounters. The interface works on the same principle — generative, not fixed. The same region of the graph, explored by three different people, produces three different readings. Each person arrives carrying different knowledge, sensitive to different gaps, weighted differently. Like the three Georgian voices: improvising within shared rules, responsive to the other parts, never identical, always recognisably the same song. And the water does not leave the surface it crosses unchanged. Each person who moves through the graph leaves a trace — a new connection, a question that wasn't there before. The commons tomorrow is not the commons today, because someone moved through it. That is what porosity means when you build it rather than just advocate for it.

The infrastructure is open. If we get it wrong, anyone can take the same data, apply different rules, and run a different commons. A knowledge commons that can be enclosed is not a commons.

Water finds the cracks. The field is full of them.
